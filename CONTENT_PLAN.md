## D2: The Reckoning (SEP 2009)

### D2-A: Enterprise Path
**Opening Moment**: A Fortune 500 CIO calls - his sales team is demanding iPhones. The IT department can block them, but for how long?

**Story Text** (~200 words):
- Open with the CIO call at 7:30 AM
- 32 months since iPhone launch, App Store has 85,000 apps
- Your enterprise fortress held - 78% of Fortune 500 still standardize on Windows Mobile
- But BYOD requests up 400%. Employees buying personal iPhones, syncing to Exchange anyway
- Android launched last year. 18 OEMs now committed
- Windows Mobile 6.5 shipped - reviewers called it "lipstick on a pig"
- The objective: How do you defend the enterprise while the consumer market slips away?

**Info Cards** (3):
1. **"Enterprise BYOD Report"** (Market Research, HIGH reliability)
   - BYOD adoption stats
   - CIO survey: 67% expect to allow personal devices within 2 years
   - Security concerns vs. employee satisfaction trade-offs

2. **"Windows Mobile 7 Status"** (Internal Memo, MEDIUM reliability)
   - Project "Photon" delays - touch UI still 18 months out
   - Internal debate: ship WM 6.6 or wait for ground-up rewrite
   - Engineering team morale concerns

3. **"Google Enterprise Push"** (Competitor Intel, MEDIUM reliability)
   - Google Apps for Business growing
   - Android Enterprise features roadmap leaked
   - "Microsoft's enterprise moat is shallower than they think"

**Options** (4):
1. **Deepen Enterprise Moat** ($300M)
   - Accelerate Exchange ActiveSync, MDM tools, security certifications
   - Risk: Become "the BlackBerry of software"
   - Upside: Lock in enterprise for 3-5 more years

2. **Hybrid Strategy** ($1.2B)
   - Enterprise security with consumer-friendly touch UI
   - Risk: Please no one, ship late
   - Upside: Best of both worlds if executed

3. **Acquire BlackBerry** ($12B)
   - Buy the other enterprise leader, consolidate
   - Risk: Two legacy platforms, culture clash
   - Upside: 60% enterprise share combined

4. **Concede & Pivot to Cloud** ($500M)
   - Accept mobile hardware loss, bet on services
   - Risk: Cede the device layer entirely
   - Upside: Azure + Office everywhere strategy

### D2-B: Consumer Path
**Opening Moment**: The KIN review embargo lifts. Engadget's headline: "Microsoft's KIN: A phone for people who don't want a smartphone."

**Story Text** (~200 words):
- Open with marketing team huddled around laptops, reading reviews
- Your $800M consumer push produced the KIN - killed after 48 days
- 503 units sold (some reports say 8,000, internal says 503)
- Meanwhile, App Store crossed 3 billion downloads
- Android went from 0 to 18 OEMs in 18 months
- Your touch-screen Windows Mobile phones exist but feel like "a desktop shrunk down"
- The objective: Do you double down on consumer, or admit the bet failed?

**Info Cards** (3):
1. **"KIN Post-Mortem"** (Internal Analysis, HIGH reliability)
   - What went wrong: carrier conflict, wrong positioning, no apps
   - $1B total investment written off
   - Team reassigned to Windows Phone project

2. **"Consumer Smartphone Trends"** (Market Research, HIGH reliability)
   - App ecosystem is the new moat
   - Carrier subsidies favor iPhone/Android
   - "The app gap is now a chasm"

3. **"OEM Partner Feedback"** (Partner Intel, MEDIUM reliability)
   - HTC, Samsung considering Android-only
   - "Microsoft's licensing fees don't match the value"
   - Demand for touch-first UI

**Options** (4):
1. **Match the Free** ($2B)
   - Make Windows Phone free to OEMs, massive developer fund
   - Risk: Destroys licensing revenue model
   - Upside: Stop OEM bleeding

2. **Go Vertical** ($3B)
   - Build your own phone, control the whole stack
   - Risk: Alienate remaining OEMs
   - Upside: Apple proved the model

3. **Premium Positioning** ($800M)
   - Accept smaller share, focus on quality/enterprise crossover
   - Risk: Android commoditizes the low end
   - Upside: Higher margins, clearer identity

4. **Strategic Retreat** (Status Quo)
   - Slow investment, focus elsewhere
   - Risk: Signal defeat to market
   - Upside: Preserve capital for cloud bet

### D2-C: Acquisition Path (Nokia Deal in Progress)
**Opening Moment**: Nokia's CEO calls from Helsinki. "The board is nervous. Android is eating us alive. We need to close this or I'm taking their call next."

**Story Text** (~200 words):
- Open with the transatlantic call, rain against Helsinki windows
- 32 months into Nokia integration talks
- Nokia's market cap down 40% since you started discussions
- Their Symbian OS losing ground daily - 600 engineers quit last quarter
- But they still ship 100M phones per quarter
- Microsoft has committed $6B to due diligence, early integration
- Android OEMs now shipping more units than Nokia
- The objective: How do you close this deal and what do you do with Nokia?

**Info Cards** (3):
1. **"Nokia Valuation Update"** (Financial Analysis, HIGH reliability)
   - Market cap: $52B (down from $68B at talks start)
   - Handset margins compressing
   - Symbian development costs still $1.2B/year

2. **"Integration Scenarios"** (Internal Strategy, MEDIUM reliability)
   - Option A: Full Windows transition (3 years)
   - Option B: Dual platform (Symbian + Windows)
   - Option C: Let Nokia keep MeeGo

3. **"OEM Reaction Forecast"** (Market Intel, LOW reliability)
   - HTC, Samsung likely to go Android-only if deal closes
   - "Microsoft becoming a competitor changes everything"
   - Some OEMs already reducing Windows Mobile SKUs

**Options** (4):
1. **Force Windows Transition** ($8B total)
   - Full acquisition, mandate Windows Phone
   - Risk: Nokia talent exodus, OEM defection
   - Upside: Vertical integration like Apple

2. **Dual Platform Strategy** ($10B total)
   - Acquire Nokia, run both Windows and MeeGo/Symbian
   - Risk: Divided resources, confused market
   - Upside: Hedge bets, preserve Nokia identity

3. **Let Nokia Lead** ($5B partnership)
   - Strategic partnership, not full acquisition
   - Risk: Less control, Nokia could still pivot
   - Upside: Preserve OEM relationships

4. **Push Nokia to Android** ($2B partnership)
   - Partner but let them use Android with Microsoft services
   - Risk: Abandon Windows Phone entirely
   - Upside: Services revenue, lower risk

### D2-D: Wait Path (Strategic Patience Tested)
**Opening Moment**: The quarterly board meeting. A director slides a chart across the table: Windows Mobile market share, 32 months of decline. "We've been patient. What now?"

**Story Text** (~200 words):
- Open with the boardroom, afternoon sun, uncomfortable silence
- You chose patience. Market chose chaos.
- Windows Mobile: 42% → 15%. Twenty-seven points gone.
- iPhone: 0% → 14%. From nothing to phenomenon.
- Android: 0% → 4%. Eighteen OEMs and accelerating.
- Your $21B cash pile is intact. But so is the problem.
- Windows Mobile 6.5 bought time. Not much.
- Competitors moved. You watched. Now what?
- The objective: The market has spoken. Do you finally act, or accept a different future?

**Info Cards** (3):
1. **"32-Month Market Analysis"** (Strategic Review, HIGH reliability)
   - Complete market share breakdown
   - App ecosystem comparison (devastating)
   - "The window for platform entry is closing"

2. **"Windows Phone 7 Readiness"** (Internal Assessment, MEDIUM reliability)
   - New platform 12-18 months from ship
   - Touch-first UI finally ready for testing
   - "Best mobile OS we've ever built" - internal reviews

3. **"Android Fork Feasibility"** (Technical Analysis, LOW reliability)
   - Could fork Android, add Microsoft services
   - Legal risks with Google unclear
   - Amazon exploring similar approach

**Options** (4):
1. **Acquire Nokia Now** ($15B)
   - Buy hardware capability, ship Windows Phone together
   - Risk: Late, expensive, integration challenge
   - Upside: Finally have end-to-end control

2. **Ship Windows Phone 7** ($1.5B)
   - Ground-up rewrite, ship in 2010
   - Risk: Starting from zero apps, zero market share
   - Upside: Clean break, modern OS

3. **Fork Android** ($800M)
   - Build Microsoft Android with Bing, Office, Xbox
   - Risk: Google retaliation, legal exposure
   - Upside: Apps on day one

4. **Concede to Services** (Status Quo)
   - Accept platform loss, focus on apps/cloud
   - Risk: No mobile presence
   - Upside: Office on iOS/Android could win

## D3: The Platform War (JAN 2011)

### Context for All D3 Variants
**Historical Anchor**: February 11, 2011 - Nokia-Microsoft partnership announced. "Two turkeys don't make an eagle" - Finnish press

### D3-P (Platform Builder Variants)

**D3-P-STANDARD: Building the Foundation**

**Opening Moment**: The Windows Phone 7 launch event just ended. 15,000 apps in the store. iPhone has 400,000. A developer in the front row asks: "Why should I build for 2% market share?"

**Story Text** (~180 words):
- Open with the developer's question echoing in the venue
- Windows Phone 7 shipped. Reviews: "Beautiful but incomplete."
- Cut/paste missing. Multitasking missing. Apps missing.
- Nokia partnership announced - the market yawned
- Nokia stock dropped 14% on the news
- But: the OS is genuinely good. Users who try it, like it.
- Android just passed Symbian globally. iOS prints money.
- The objective: How do you build an ecosystem from scratch?

**Info Cards** (3):
1. **"WP7 Launch Analysis"** (Market Research, HIGH)
   - 15,000 apps vs 400,000 on iOS
   - 2% market share after 90 days
   - "Beautiful for its time, but time moves fast"

2. **"Nokia Partnership Terms"** (Internal, MEDIUM)
   - $1B annual platform support payment from Nokia
   - Nokia commits to Windows Phone exclusively
   - 3-year transition timeline

3. **"Developer Sentiment Survey"** (Developer Relations, MEDIUM)
   - "Show me the users first"
   - 80% developing for iOS, 65% Android, 12% Windows Phone
   - App gap psychological as much as real

**Options** (3):
1. **Massive Developer Bet** ($2B)
   - Pay developers to build, guarantee revenue
   - Risk: Mercenary apps, quality issues
   - Upside: Close the app gap faster

2. **Acquire webOS** ($1.2B)
   - Buy HP's mobile OS, talent, patents
   - Risk: Another platform to merge
   - Upside: Engineering talent, Palm patents

3. **Focus on Differentiation** ($500M)
   - Accept fewer apps, win on unique features
   - Risk: "Different" isn't always better
   - Upside: Clear positioning, sustainable
